Lesson 4 · 麻将文化

Culture & Variants

Sichuan mahjong is not "mahjong with some rules removed". It is a distinct local game with its own logic, its own etiquette and its own temperament — and knowing that makes the rules much easier to remember.

1. One game, many families

Mahjong 麻将 emerged in China in the 19th century and spread outward from the Yangtze delta. The tile set was standardised into roughly its modern 136-tile form in the 1920s, and regional families diverged from there — Cantonese mahjong kept a large fan system, Japanese riichi added its own scoring and a formal league structure, and Sichuan mahjong went the other way entirely.

Where most variants added tiles and bonus patterns over time, Sichuan mahjong subtracted:

2. Why it is shaped like this

Sichuan mahjong is played above all in Sichuan province — Chengdu is its spiritual home, and the game is inseparable from the city's teahouse culture 茶馆. Tables sit in teahouses all day; people play for hours, in conversation, with the tiles clacking and the discards being called out loud.

That setting explains the design. Teahouse play rewards speed and punishes ceremony:

  • Stripping the honours removes a lot of slow, low-information decisions — every tile is now part of the same three-suit puzzle.
  • Forcing everyone to discard a whole suit (定缺) front-loads a real decision into the first ten seconds, then removes a third of the thinking for the rest of the round.
  • Letting three players win (血战到底) means a round rarely fizzles out, so there is almost always something worth playing for.

The temperament of the game

Sichuan mahjong is direct and unforgiving about inefficiency. There is no safe, patient "wait and see" mode: if you do not clear your missing suit and climb toward 下叫 (ready) quickly, the round will simply end around you and you will pay the penalties. Locals describe the style as fast, blunt and unsentimental — and the rules genuinely encode that.

3. Variants you will meet

VariantChineseHow it differs
Battle to the end血战到底 The standard modern game, and what this site teaches: winners drop out, up to three win, then penalties if the wall runs dry.
Blood flows like a river血流成河 The extreme version — after you win you keep playing and can win again and again in the same round. Scores get very large, rounds get long, and the whole feel changes.
Chengdu / Chongqing house rules成都 / 重庆麻将 Small differences in fan values, kong payments and how strictly the missing suit is policed. This is why every table argues about numbers before it starts.

If you sit down at a real table, the first thing you will hear is a negotiation about exactly these values. See Scoring for the preset this site uses and how to read it as one choice among several.

4. Table etiquette

  1. Declare your missing suit out loud before the first tile is discarded. Everyone must know what you are throwing away — it is public information, and hiding it is not a thing.
  2. Announce your discard by name as you throw it (三万, 五筒…). The call is part of the game, not noise. It is also how you learn to read tiles fast.
  3. Call your claims clearly — 碰 for pong, 杠 for kong, 自摸 for a self-drawn win. Call before you touch the tiles.
  4. Keep the pace up. Slow play is the one thing Sichuan tables genuinely do not tolerate.
  5. Hands off the wall. You draw your own tile and no one else's.

Small phrases that get you through a table

碰 pèng — pong · 杠 gàng — kong · 胡 hú — win · 自摸 zì mō — self-drawn win · 定缺 dìng quē — declare missing suit · 幺鸡 yāo jī — the 1 of bamboo. The full list is in the glossary.

5. A note on stakes

Sichuan mahjong is usually played socially, in teahouses and at home, sometimes for very small stakes — it is a social ritual as much as a competition. Gambling is strictly regulated in mainland China, and none of the tools on this site involve money: the scoring engine exists so you can learn the arithmetic of a hand and see why a win is worth what it is worth. Play it as a game of skill and shape, which is what it is.
