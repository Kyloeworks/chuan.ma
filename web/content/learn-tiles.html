Reading the Tiles

You cannot play a game you cannot read. The good news: Sichuan mahjong has the smallest tile set of any major variant — 108 tiles, three suits, nine numbers. Learn nine characters and one visual trap, and you can name any tile on the table in under a second.

1. A deliberately stripped deck

If you have seen Hong Kong or Japanese mahjong, you are used to extra tiles: winds 東南西北, dragons 中發白, flowers and seasons. Sichuan mahjong throws all of them away.

What is left is three suits of nine ranks, four copies of each tile:

SuitChinesePinyinWhat you actually seeMnemonic
Characters / myriads万wàn a Chinese numeral sitting above the character 萬 万 means "ten thousand" — the money suit
Bamboo / sticks条tiáo that many bamboo sticks drawn on the tile 条 means "strip" or "stick"
Dots / circles筒tǒng that many circles drawn on the tile 筒 means "tube" or "barrel"

3 suits × 9 ranks × 4 copies = 108 tiles. That is the entire game.

Why the deck is small, and why that matters

With no winds or dragons to get in the way, almost every hand is made of the same raw material: runs and triplets inside three suits. Sichuan mahjong is therefore much purer than other variants — it is almost entirely a test of shape efficiency. See the basics for how that plays out.

2. The nine numerals

Every tile is identified by a Chinese numeral. These are the everyday forms — the ceremonial accounting forms (壹貳叁肆伍陆柒捌玖) belong on bank documents, not on tiles.

On the bamboo and dot suits you may not even need the numerals: the tile simply shows that many sticks or circles. On the character suit the numeral is written out, which is where these nine characters earn their keep.

3. The one tile that is not what it looks like

1 Bamboo 一条 is drawn as a bird

Every other bamboo tile shows its rank as a count of sticks — except the 1. Traditionally it is drawn as a bird (a peacock or sparrow), not as a single stick. Players call it 幺鸡 (yāo jī, literally "little chicken").

So if you are scanning a hand for "one bamboo", stop looking for a lone stick. The bird is the one. This single piece of tile art has confused more beginners than any rule in the game.

4. Saying a tile out loud

The pattern is always [number][suit] — number first, suit second. That is the opposite of how English speakers tend to guess.

TileChinesePinyinMeans
三万三 + 万sān wàn3 of characters
七条七 + 条qī tiáo7 of bamboo
五筒五 + 筒wǔ tǒng5 of dots
幺鸡幺 + 鸡yāo jī1 of bamboo (the bird)
九万九 + 万jiǔ wàn9 of characters

Chinese mahjong tables are loud partly because discards are announced: you say the tile name as you throw it. Learn these five phrases and you can follow the conversation at a real table.

A quick naming convention you will hear constantly: because the rank comes first, a hand sorted from 1 to 9 in one suit reads as a list of numbers — 1条 2条 3条 and so on. Sorted hands are also announced by their shape, e.g. "I have 1-2-3 bamboo", which is how you will start hearing your own hands.

5. The real skill: sorting a hand fast

Naming tiles is table stakes. What actually makes you a player is the first three seconds of every round: sorting 13 tiles into their three suits and seeing which suit is thinnest.

  1. Split into three piles — characters left, bamboo middle, dots right (or whatever order you like, as long as it never changes).
  2. Order each pile 1 → 9 so runs and pairs jump out at you.
  3. Count each pile. The smallest pile is your natural missing suit 定缺 — the suit you will throw away entirely this round.

Doing this reflexively is worth more than any strategy tip on this site. If you have to think about it, you will lose the first three turns of every round.

6. Three drills you can do alone

DrillWhat to doTarget
Name the tile Shuffle the 27 distinct tile faces and name each one out loud as you flip it. 27 tiles, no mistakes
Speed sort Deal yourself 13 random tiles and sort them into three ordered suits. Under 10 seconds
Call the missing suit From a sorted 13-tile hand, say out loud which suit you would drop — without pausing. Instant, every time
